Earlier than Hinton, Deeper than Kaiming He: Why Did He Keep Missing Out on “Greatness”?

Cris Yan · Jian Yan May 13, 2026 Originally published on WeChat ↗
中文 English

While looking back at the history of deep learning, I kept coming across a name I had barely heard before: Jürgen Schmidhuber. He invented LSTM. He used GPUs to train deep convolutional neural networks before Hinton—the “godfather of deep learning”—Ilya, and Alex did. He also made extensive use of residual-like architectures before Kaiming He, training neural networks with more than a hundred layers. So why has he not become a godfather figure enshrined in the deep learning pantheon alongside Hinton and Kaiming He?

1. An excessive pursuit of academic rigor.

As a German computer scientist, Schmidhuber places enormous importance on logical rigor. His papers are highly inspiring and forward-looking. As early as the 1990s, he proposed a precursor to something resembling an attention mechanism, albeit without nonlinear activation functions and other elements at the time. His ideas inspired a generation of deep learning researchers, including Ilya and the founders of DeepMind. Yet, as a scholar devoted to pure research, he seemed less concerned with the subsequent engineering implementation once he had pointed out a promising direction. For example, in Training Very Deep Networks, which uses gating mechanisms for residual connections, he proposed a way to train networks a hundred layers deep. But it did not make a splash on ImageNet, largely because the gating mechanisms introduced too many parameters, making optimization and training extremely difficult.

2. Exceptional ability, coupled with an aloof personality.

Supremely confident, Schmidhuber did not see fit to band together with the “big three” of deep learning—Hinton, LeCun, and Bengio—even when neural networks were at their lowest ebb. Instead, he pursued his research independently within a small European circle. The big three, by contrast, weathered the neural network winter together by citing one another’s papers, securing projects, and engaging in deep discussions.

3. A lack of storytelling skill.

Hinton and LeCun excel at explaining what AI does to a general audience through plain language and analogies. Schmidhuber, on the other hand, tends toward exceptionally rigorous, mathematical language. For what is almost the same concept, for instance, Hinton chose the straightforward, intuitive name “Dropout,” while Schmidhuber called his work “Market-based credit assignment in a network of evolving adaptive reinforcement learners.” From a communications standpoint, the latter was practically dead on arrival.

Schmidhuber is undoubtedly a great scholar in deep learning. But history and society tend to reward those whose work has the most direct impact on human progress at the largest scale. From the breakthrough of AlexNet to that of ResNet, he seems never to have fully understood why the world has not given him the recognition he seeks. Since 2015, he has continued to criticize the big three on Twitter and his blog.

This leaves us with a question: how can we contribute as much as possible to human progress? One answer might be to produce simple, reusable work; express ideas clearly; keep an open mind; and embrace collaboration. Ultimately, our value must be reflected in the tangible contributions we make to others.
