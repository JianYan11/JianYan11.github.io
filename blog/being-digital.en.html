Being Digital: How a Book from 1995 Anticipated Recommendation Algorithms, Starlink, and AI

Cris Yan · Jian Yan April 27, 2026 Updated April 28, 2026

Originally published in Chinese on the WeChat account “颜简的学习笔记” (Jian Yan’s Study Notes). Original on WeChat ↗

Reading Being Digital, a book written in 1996 that largely anticipated what would happen over the following twenty years, once again convinced me that history converges on a particular trajectory under the pressure of basic human needs and advances in productive capacity. In other words, predicting the broad direction of history is entirely possible.

On this page

  1. 1. Why digital signals are far better than analog signals
  2. 2. The physical world and the world of bits
  3. 3. Why search engines and recommendation engines are inevitable

1. Why digital signals are far better than analog signals

In my analog electronics class, the instructor said that digital and analog electronics are equals, with neither inherently better than the other. I think that is wildly wrong. Analog signals cannot be stored or processed through programmable computation, and they suffer greater losses in transmission. Across the three essential operations on signals—computation (transforming signals), communication, and storage—digital signals are better.

In the early days, around 1990, digital sampling rates were insufficient and digital-to-analog conversion was inefficient, so people still missed the advantages of analog television. But digital signals are an entirely different species. Bits can readily be processed, stored, and combined. That means television can be available on demand: apart from events such as sports matches, programs do not need to be broadcast live all the time. Since bits can be stored, the range of available content can expand enormously. Television should also be personalized for each viewer, because bits can be searched and processed. And it should be able to present every kind of medium—text, video, and sound—because all media are bits.

Does that sound familiar? Yes: once signals become bits, television inevitably evolves into a computer. The boundary between the two gradually disappears.

2. The physical world and the world of bits

The author gives an interesting example to explain the difference between bits and atoms. At a computing conference, he and scholars from around the world were discussing the future of the internet while still drinking Evian—the European glacier water that costs about twenty yuan a bottle. Shipping a bottle of Evian from the Alps to the conference takes weeks and costs a great deal, whereas sending information from the venue thousands of miles away consumes almost no energy.

This led him to reflect that information has no mass, color, taste, or physical form. It travels at the speed of light, and transforming it consumes almost no energy.

Atoms have weight. Moving them requires energy; rearranging them requires energy; storing them takes up space.

So in the future, whenever all we need to transfer is information, we should not have to do it by moving atoms. Think of an 8 a.m. class: if all you want is to acquire knowledge, why do you have to be there in person?

Bits will make distance disappear. Two people may be half a world apart, but any two computers should effectively have no distance between them.

3. Why search engines and recommendation engines are inevitable

Consider the supply of and demand for bits. As the supply keeps growing, the amount of information people need in everyday life does not increase nearly as quickly. Inevitably, people need help finding the bits they want among all the others.

Bits also have a useful property: we can compute bits about bits. That helps people quickly find what they want in a vast collection of bits—the search engine.

In communications, supply and demand can also be understood as a transmitter and a receiver. (Speaking as a communications engineering student!) As the transmitter sends more and more signals, the receiver inevitably has to become selective. Which signals should it accept? The ones the user needs, of course. This is the content distribution at which ByteDance excels.

I feel certain that Zhang Yiming has read this book. The understanding of information aggregation and distribution that he shows in interviews is remarkably consistent with its ideas.

To take the argument a bold step further: large AI models will increasingly transform bits into other bits—from text to video, audio, or more text. The supply of information will continue to grow exponentially, and most internet content may eventually be created by AI. How, then, will people get the bits they actually want? The answer must involve something that understands me better. My guess is a personal AI assistant: one that knows me far better than a recommendation engine does, and can tell, when I look down during a conversation, that I no longer want to continue the topic.
