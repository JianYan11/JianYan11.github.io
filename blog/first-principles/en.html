First Principles of Large Language Models

Cris Yan · Jian YanOctober 25, 2026

Large language models may look complicated, but their basic training objective is straightforward: predict the next token from the preceding context. A token is, roughly speaking, a basic unit into which text is divided.

Starting from that objective, I think two questions are worth understanding: what does accurate prediction mean? And why do larger models, more data, and more compute usually make prediction more accurate?

Let’s start with the first question.

Suppose the next token that actually appears is “Beijing,” and the model assigns it a probability of p. The loss for this prediction is:

ℓ=−log2⁡p

The higher the probability assigned to the correct answer, the lower the loss. A probability of 1/2 gives a loss of 1; a probability of 1/8 gives a loss of 3. Training keeps adjusting the model so that the content that actually occurs receives a higher predicted probability.

This equation also connects to information theory: when we encode text using the model’s probabilities, prediction loss corresponds to the ideal code length.

Predictable content takes fewer bits to describe. For example, ten thousand repetitions of “hello” can simply be described as “repeat hello ten thousand times.” Once we find the pattern, we no longer need to record every word. Patterns in language are more complex, but the same idea applies: the more accurately a model predicts text, the more effectively it can help us compress that text.

Learning patterns in language, improving prediction accuracy, and compressing information are therefore directly connected.

Now for the second question: how much can a model improve when we increase training resources?

In 2020, Kaplan and colleagues found stable relationships, across a substantial range of scales, between a language model’s prediction loss and its size, training data, and compute. These are known as neural scaling laws. Read the paper ↗

For data size, one common expression is:

L(D)=Lmin+ADα

Here, D is the amount of training data, L(D) is the average prediction loss, and Lmin is the loss floor the model approaches under the given conditions. A and α determine the shape of the curve and must be estimated through experiments.

The idea is simple: more data leads to lower loss, but further reductions in loss require progressively more data.

What makes this useful is predictability. Researchers can train a set of small models, measure the trend, and estimate the results of scaling up. That helps them decide how many resources to invest.

Model size, data, and compute also need to grow together. The Chinchilla study showed that, under the conditions it investigated, increasing model parameters should be accompanied by more training data to use compute more efficiently. A model with a large capacity needs enough training to make use of it. Read the paper ↗

The two equations play different roles. The first directly connects prediction to information encoding; the second summarizes scaling patterns observed in experiments. Information theory helps us understand what the model is optimizing, while scaling laws help us estimate how much improvement a larger investment can deliver.

For me, this offers a starting point for understanding large language models: models learn patterns from data and use those patterns to reduce uncertainty in prediction. More resources take that process further; better methods may let the same resources learn more.

Experiments can tell us how far prediction loss can fall. How much that improvement translates into reasoning, programming, or solving practical problems still needs to be evaluated separately.
